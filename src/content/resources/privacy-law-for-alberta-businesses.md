If your business collects names, emails, phone numbers, health details or payment information, Canadian privacy law applies to you. Here is a plain-language overview for Alberta businesses. *This article is general information, not legal advice — speak to a lawyer about your specific situation.*

## Which law applies: PIPA or PIPEDA?

- **Alberta's Personal Information Protection Act (PIPA)** generally applies to private-sector organizations handling personal information within Alberta, including employee information.
- **The federal Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act (PIPEDA)** applies to federally regulated businesses (such as banks, airlines and telecoms) and to personal information that crosses provincial or national borders in the course of commercial activity.
- **Health information** held by health custodians such as physicians and pharmacists is covered by Alberta's **Health Information Act (HIA)**.

Many businesses deal with more than one of these — for example, an Alberta company with customers in other provinces.

## What the laws expect

While the details differ, the core principles are similar:

1. **Consent** — collect, use and disclose personal information only for reasonable purposes people have agreed to.
2. **Limit collection** — collect only what you need.
3. **Safeguards** — protect information with security appropriate to its sensitivity.
4. **Accuracy and access** — keep it accurate and let people see and correct their information.
5. **Accountability** — name someone responsible for privacy and have written policies.
6. **Retention** — keep information only as long as needed, then destroy it securely.

## Data breaches: what you must do

Both laws include mandatory breach reporting:

- **Under PIPA**, an organization must notify the Office of the Information and Privacy Commissioner of Alberta without unreasonable delay when a breach creates a real risk of significant harm to an individual, and the Commissioner may require you to notify affected individuals.
- **Under PIPEDA**, breaches that create a real risk of significant harm must be reported to the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada and affected individuals must be notified. You must also **keep a record of every breach** — even minor ones — for at least 24 months.

"Significant harm" can include identity theft, financial loss, humiliation and damage to reputation or relationships.

## How IT security supports compliance

The "safeguards" requirement is where your IT matters most. Regulators expect reasonable protections such as:

- Multi-factor authentication and strong access controls
- Encryption of laptops and sensitive data
- Up-to-date systems and endpoint protection
- Secure, tested backups
- Logs that show who accessed what
- Staff training

## Using service providers

You remain responsible for personal information even when a provider (such as your IT company or a cloud service) handles it for you. Use written agreements that require providers to protect the information, and tell people when their information may be processed outside Canada.
